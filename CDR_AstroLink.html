1.2. Mission objectives

The AstroLink CanSat mission focuses on measuring air quality parameters at different atmospheric layers during descent. Our primary scientific objective is to create a vertical profile of particulate matter concentration from approximately 1 km altitude down to ground level.

Primary Mission

Measure atmospheric temperature and pressure during descent to calculate altitude and descent rate.

Secondary Mission

Investigate the vertical distribution of airborne particulate matter (PM1.0, PM2.5, PM4.0, PM10) using the Sensirion SPS30 sensor. This data will help understand how air pollution varies with altitude and can provide insights into:

  • Particulate matter concentration gradients in the lower atmosphere
  • The relationship between altitude and air quality
  • Potential sources of pollution at different atmospheric layers
  • Correlation between meteorological conditions (temperature, humidity) and particle distribution

Success Criteria

  • Successfully deploy from the launch vehicle and descend under parachute
  • Collect continuous sensor data throughout the flight (temperature, pressure, particulate matter, GPS position)
  • Transmit real-time telemetry to the ground station via LoRa
  • Store all flight data onboard for post-flight analysis
  • Safely land and be recovered intact
  • Remain operational for at least 4 hours after landing

2. CANSAT DESCRIPTION

2.1. Overview of the mission

The AstroLink CanSat will be launched on a rocket to an altitude of approximately 1 km, where it will be ejected and descend under a parachute system at a rate of 8-11 m/s. During descent, it will continuously measure atmospheric parameters using its sensor suite and transmit data to the ground station via LoRa radio link while simultaneously storing all data onboard. The satellite will remain active for at least 4 hours after landing to facilitate recovery.

Critical Components and Systems

  • Microcontroller: ESP32-S3 (dual-core, WiFi capable)
  • Primary sensors: BMP585 (pressure/temperature), GPS module (M100-Pro HGLRC)
  • Secondary mission sensor: SPS30 particulate matter sensor
  • Supporting sensors: BNO085 IMU (orientation), SCD40 (CO2/humidity), QMC5883L (magnetometer)
  • Communication: LoRa radio (SX1278 at 433.2 MHz, with option for 868 MHz ISM band)
  • Power: LiPo battery with voltage regulation
  • Recovery: Parachute system

2.3. Electrical design

The electrical system is built around the ESP32-S3 microcontroller, which provides sufficient processing power, memory, and connectivity options for our mission requirements.

Microcontroller Specifications

  • MCU: ESP32-S3-DevKitC-1 N16R8
  • Processor: Dual-core Xtensa LX7 @ 240 MHz
  • RAM: 512 KB SRAM + 8 MB PSRAM (external)
  • Flash: 16 MB (quad SPI)
  • Interfaces: SPI, I2C, UART, GPIO

Power System

  • Battery: Cellevia CL-18650-29E/1S1P (Li-Ion 18650 cell)
  • Nominal Voltage: 3.7V
  • Capacity: 2670 mAh (2.67 Ah)
  • Energy: 9.75 Wh
  • Voltage Regulator: 3.3V LDO for MCU and most sensors, 5V boost converter for SPS30

GPS Navigation Module

Navigation is handled by the HGLRC M100_MINI GPS module, selected for its compact form factor and performance characteristics suitable for high-altitude applications.

System Architecture:

  • Processing Speed: The ESP32-S3 dual-core @ 240 MHz effortlessly manages the GPS module's high 10 Hz update rate alongside concurrent data acquisition tasks.
  • Hardware UART: Dedicated hardware serial ports (HardwareSerial) enable stable communication with the GPS module without software-induced overhead.
  • Space Efficiency: Manual integration allowed for flexible component placement within the cylindrical chassis, optimizing the Center of Gravity (CoG).

Hardware Integration:

The system was implemented using point-to-point wiring (perfboard) rather than a custom PCB. While this approach facilitated rapid prototyping and iteration, it demanded heightened attention to mechanical protection.

Assembly and Vibration Proofing:

  • Wiring: Multi-stranded silicone wires were used instead of solid-core wires to prevent fatigue-induced snapping.
  • Insulation: All solder joints are insulated with heat-shrink tubing to prevent short circuits.
  • Securing Components: Modules and wire harnesses are secured to the CanSat internal frame using hot glue or cable ties for effective strain relief.

Software Implementation (ESP32-S3 Specific):

  • Protocol: UBX binary protocol (u-blox proprietary) for efficient, low-overhead communication
  • Baud Rate: Serial port initialized at 115,200 bps, matching the GPS module's default configuration
  • Data Buffering: The ESP32-S3's ample SRAM allows for buffering UBX messages without data loss

Flight Profile and Operating Environment:

The HGLRC M100_MINI's specifications (50 km altitude, 500 m/s velocity limits) comfortably exceed the CanSat competition requirements. The primary concern for manual soldering is the mechanical load (G-forces) during liftoff.

  • Testing Procedure: A rigorous "Shake Test" is performed to verify that solder joints remain intact and the GPS fix is not lost due to intermittent connector movement.

Pre-Flight Checklist:

  • Antenna Orientation: The ceramic patch antenna faces upward, clear of metal components or the ESP32's EMI shield.
  • Cable Management: Wires are neatly routed and secured, ensuring no interference with the parachute deployment mechanism.
  • LED Status: Upon power-up, the ESP32 status LED and the GPS PPS LED (blinking blue/green) must confirm a successful boot-up and a 3D Fix.

2.4. Software design

The CanSat firmware is developed using the ESP-IDF framework with FreeRTOS for real-time task management. The software architecture is designed for reliability, modularity, and efficient data handling.

Development Environment

  • Framework: ESP-IDF (Espressif IoT Development Framework)
  • Build System: PlatformIO
  • Programming Language: C/C++
  • RTOS: FreeRTOS

Software Architecture

The firmware uses a multi-task architecture where each sensor has its own dedicated task running at an appropriate priority and frequency:

Task Priority Frequency Purpose
gps_task 8 ~1 Hz GPS UBX protocol parsing, position/velocity data
sps30_task 10 ~1 Hz Particulate matter measurement (secondary mission)
bno085_task 5 50 Hz IMU quaternion, acceleration, gyroscope
bmp585_task 6 10 Hz Pressure, temperature, altitude calculation
scd40_task 6 1 Hz CO2, temperature, humidity
lora_task 7 Variable Telemetry transmission to ground station

On-Board Data Handling (OBDH)

The system uses a compact 64-byte binary record format to store flight data efficiently:

Data Field Size (bytes) Description
Timestamp 4 Unix time or uptime in seconds
GPS data 20 Latitude, longitude, altitude, velocity, fix type, satellites
Orientation 10 Quaternion (i, j, k, w) and accuracy status
Magnetometer 8 X, Y, Z raw values and computed heading
BMP585 8 Pressure (Pa), temperature, barometric altitude
SCD40 6 CO2 (ppm), temperature, humidity
SPS30 8 PM1.0, PM2.5, PM4.0, PM10 concentrations
Total 64 Per sample record size

Data Storage

  • PSRAM buffer: Up to 86,400 records (24 hours at 1 Hz) in ring buffer
  • LittleFS: Persistent storage of flight recordings as binary files
  • Estimated data per flight: ~64 bytes × 120 seconds = ~7.5 KB (descent only), plus pre/post flight data

Battery Life Calculation

Battery: Cellevia CL-18650-29E/1S1P

  • Capacity: 2670 mAh
  • Energy: 9.75 Wh
  • Nominal voltage: 3.7V

Power consumption analysis:

  • Total system power (from table above): ~1250 mW peak
  • Average power (considering LoRa TX duty cycle ~10%): ~930 mW
  • Average current at 3.7V: 930 mW / 3.7V = ~251 mA

Estimated runtime:

  • Runtime = Capacity / Average current
  • Runtime = 2670 mAh / 251 mA = ~10.6 hours
  • With 80% efficiency margin: ~8.5 hours

Conclusion: The battery provides more than double the required 4-hour minimum operating time, ensuring sufficient margin for the mission and post-landing recovery operations.
